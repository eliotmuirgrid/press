# Extremes Change the Rules

When you push anything to its limits—whether it’s speed, size, efficiency, or complexity—the rules we've always relied on start to break down or shift in unexpected ways. That’s precisely why I often explore extremes: by venturing far beyond the ordinary, we uncover new possibilities—and new problems—that would otherwise remain invisible.

Take science, for example:

- **Extreme speed and mass:** At everyday speeds and sizes, Newton’s laws make perfect sense. But as you approach the speed of light or deal with immense masses like black holes, Newtonian physics crumbles and Einstein’s theory of relativity takes over. Space and time themselves start to behave differently. The rules change.
  
- **The extremely small:** At the scale of atoms and subatomic particles, classical mechanics gives way to quantum mechanics. Suddenly, uncertainty, probability, and wave-particle duality are fundamental—not optional. Once again, the rules change.

But this phenomenon isn’t limited to physics. In technology, when we push systems to their extremes—performance, capability, automation—the “rules” for developers, users, and entire industries shift dramatically.

Consider **advanced packaging tools** in software. Imagine a tool so advanced that you simply point it at any application and it packages the entire environment seamlessly—no manual setup, no pain. The whole process becomes almost magical, like something out of *Tron*.

When something complex and difficult suddenly becomes simple and fast, the playing field changes for everyone. **Barriers drop**, new opportunities appear, but so do new risks:

- **Upside:** More people can build and innovate. Progress accelerates. Efficiency skyrockets.
- **Downside:** New vulnerabilities emerge. Old ways of thinking—or even entire skillsets—become obsolete. We can’t assume what used to work will still apply (“we’re not in Kansas anymore, Dorothy”). Unintended consequences are a real risk.

## The Case for Extremes

Pushing to extremes always changes the landscape. It brings not just dramatic progress, but also uncertainty and risk, because old assumptions no longer hold.

I’ve seen this firsthand in my own company. At one point, we had 20 people doing what, with the right automation, should have taken only one. By tearing down that inefficient structure—despite the short-term pain of reduced revenue and layoffs—we built something leaner and far more effective. Operating out of a tax-neutral jurisdiction like the Cayman Islands, our overall business performance jumped. While cash flow dipped briefly, my post-tax income is now projected to at least double compared to the old, broken model.

It takes courage to embrace extreme change. But ultimately, the rewards go to those willing to make bold choices. Yes, jobs were shed—but these were not value-creating roles. It was necessary, and ultimately transformative.

**Pushing to extremes rewrites the rules—not just in science and technology, but in business and beyond. The brave reap the rewards, while those who [cling to the status quo are eliminated](/system/remove).**
