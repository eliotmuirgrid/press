# Leverage

Leverage is all about identifying, in the never-ending complexity of the world, *where* that one clever nudge will get you the most spectacular, drama-inducing result for the least possible effort. It’s the art of the “best bang for your buck.” Where’s the button that, when pressed, dominoes everything else?

Here are some examples of system leverage:

## Eliot learning how to write good

(that is a deliberate joke)

Leadership is, at its heart, basically communication wearing a business suit. If you can’t get your big ideas out of your head and elegantly into someone else’s, your career trajectory is roughly that of a lead balloon.

## Eliot learning to touch type

Discovering touch typing was Eliot’s first “unlock.” Suddenly, communication got faster. The internal monologue—now in stereo and at 120 wpm—had a chance of escaping the bottleneck of two-finger henpecking. Whole worlds of emails, memos, and overlong Slack messages opened up.

## Eliot learning how to use voice-to-text

Then came voice-to-text. Communicating with Eliot at this point became a little like discussing groceries with a caffeinated auctioneer: words flying past at alarming speeds, punctuation sometimes an optional extra, but *oh, the productivity!* Warning: sustained exposure may cause mild whiplash in friends and colleagues.

Eliot found that applying this superpower when jokingly discussing his (fictional) plans to make a virus to help clean up Cayman’s feral cat population resulted in him being ejected quickly from the neighborhood WhatsApp group.

It's okay—Eliot plans to apply [Flow Communicate](/flow/communicate) to help reconnect with all those neighbors in good time. Flow Communicate will slow down Eliot's natural communication speed. If they still feel overwhelmed, they always have the option of using [Flow Protect](/flow/protect) to avoid unwanted communication from Eliot after that. It's lovely how ideas and innovation spread and the whole community benefits, moving towards a safe, trust-based model of communication—even if they don't love the creative mind that helped them get there!

## Eliot learning how to get AI to clean up the mess

Of course, with all that rapid output, there’s… debris. Enter AI: the ever-patient, never-sarcastic ghostwriter, sweeping up Eliot’s typos and run-ons, and occasionally suggesting better metaphors. Suddenly, readable prose appeared where enthusiastic chaos once reigned.

## Eliot using Flow to make it easier to write code 

Next up: tackling actual “work.” Here, the [Flow framework](/flow) flexes its muscles. Sure, Eliot’s hands are flying, his voice is dictating, and AI is proofing, but if the underlying code-writing system handles all the grindingly repetitive stuff with 100% deterministic code that's significantly better than using AI which can sometimes behave like a humourous random number generator. 

It's little bit like the difference between a 100% reliable paid professional sex worker who can be relied upon 100%
of the time to deliver service versus Eliot's string of 'partners' who while providing a lot of enjoyable fun have thus
so far not proven terribly reliable at all.  Probably an experience most humans - male and female can relate to.  Incidentally

was one of the first 'professional groups'.  Part of a time honoured tradition of human value exchange.  See [Flow Money](/flow/money).

## Screen Capture Technology

This was the plot twist. If you have screen capture, you don’t need superhuman developers—just humans who can locate and click buttons. Suddenly, your contributor pool is everyone who can find their way around a desktop (which, let’s face it, makes them more capable than many medieval wizards).

Modern computer interfaces are often mysterious, and how better to navigate this wondrous mess than to leverage the collective wisdom of all the poor, suffering individuals who have to click through all that mess?

They have both the knowledge and the incentive to contribute, so they don't have to put up with all that crap.

## The One Ring in Lord of the Rings

Sauron, that notorious early adopter of leverage, really put all his eggs—er, dark powers—into one ring-shaped basket. Top marks for centralizing power, minus a few for failing “Basic System Resilience 101.” Spoiler: Single points of failure are fun until some hobbit gets lucky. Imagine if Sauron had embraced first principles, backups, and perhaps a nice Kanban board. Who knows? Middle-earth might still be having orc management seminars to this day.
